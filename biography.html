MY LIFE STORY AND JOURNEY

I was born in Kangundo, Ndunduni, Kikuvi Village, into a family of six, consisting of my parents, two sisters, one brother, and myself. My childhood was far from easy, and there were times when I did not know whether I would ever get the opportunity to finish school. I grew up in a family where my father struggled with alcoholism, and this brought many challenges into our home. As children, we experienced difficult moments and uncertainty, but somehow we continued moving forward. Life taught us to survive with what we had and to appreciate every little opportunity that came our way. With time, my father made one of the most important decisions of his life: he quit alcohol and focused on providing for his family. He began working hard to make sure that we were raised and given the necessities we needed. Later, he turned to Christianity, and his faith brought a new sense of direction and enlightenment into our family. Watching that transformation taught me that people can change and that a difficult past does not necessarily determine someone's future. The story of my father became one of the earliest lessons in my life about second chances, responsibility, faith, and determination.

With that background, I started my education with faith and hope, although I was never certain what the future would look like. I enrolled at Matungulu Primary School, where my academic journey began. However, my early years in school were not straightforward. When I reached Class Two, I developed a habit of refusing to go to school. Instead of entering the classroom, I would sometimes go and hide in the bushes and wait for the other pupils so that I could walk home with them. At the time, I did not understand how much this behavior would affect my education. Eventually, my mother discovered what I had been doing, and I received a very serious punishment at home. She then decided that she would personally accompany me to school. My Class Two teacher was Madam Mbithi, a teacher I genuinely feared. One day, after the issue became known, she instructed one of my friends to hold my hand and leg, and I found myself lifted into the air while being punished. I was thoroughly caned, and in that moment, out of fear and desperation, I even promised that I would marry a white lady if only they would leave me alone. It may sound funny when I tell the story today, but at that age, the experience was frightening and deeply affected me.

The experience became so difficult for me that by the end of the year, I repeated Class Two. Part of me was afraid of progressing to Class Three because I would still be under the same teacher, and I felt that continuing in that environment would affect me even more. When I eventually moved forward, however, I felt as though a heavy burden had been lifted from me. I began to feel better, and life continued with its own challenges and opportunities. That experience became one of the earliest difficult chapters of my education, but it also taught me something about resilience. I learned that even when a situation feels unbearable, it eventually comes to an end. From that point, I continued with my education and gradually became more focused. I went through the different stages of primary school, facing the normal ups and downs of childhood and education. Eventually, I completed my primary education in 2018. When the results were announced, I had scored 318 marks and became number three in my class. I remember feeling extremely happy and proud of myself because, considering where I had started, the achievement meant a lot to me. It was proof that I could overcome difficulties and still perform well.

However, even my success in primary school came with an unexpected and embarrassing experience. As other pupils began receiving admission letters from schools of their choice, I noticed that something was different about mine. Because of the way my name begins, MBEKE MUTUA, some schools apparently assumed that I was a girl. Instead of receiving admission letters from boys' schools, I ended up being associated with famous girls' schools within the county. Imagine walking around and having your friends tell you, "I found a letter for you; you have been called to a girls' school." For me, it was embarrassing because I was a boy being called to a female institution. My name had created confusion that I had never expected — but I'm proudly carrying two African names; at least my names aren't colonised, thank you very much. Eventually, my father took responsibility and went out to search for a suitable secondary school for me. He came back with an admission letter for Kangundo High School, and that became the institution where I continued my secondary education.

Life at Kangundo High School was not easy. Secondary school exposed me to a different level of hardship, responsibility, discipline, and independence. There were moments when I struggled, but looking back, I am grateful for that experience because Kangundo High School taught me lessons that could not have been learned from books alone. It taught me about hardship, survival, discipline, friendships, competition, and what it means to stand on your own as a young man in society. Those years shaped my character and prepared me for the challenges that would come later in life. In 2022, I sat for my KCSE examinations. Although I did not achieve the grade I had hoped for, I refused to allow the result to define my future. I decided to return to the classroom in 2023, this time joining Katwanyaa Secondary School, with the aim of improving my performance. I spent another year studying and working toward a better result. At the end of that year, I left Katwanyaa with a Grade C. It was not exactly the result I had dreamed of, but it was another lesson in perseverance: sometimes the path to your destination takes longer than expected.

After secondary school, my ambitions began changing. At one point, I had a strong desire to venture into the medical field and imagined myself working in a hospital and helping people. But even before then, I had grown up around old computers at home and gadgets that were always within reach, though I never really knew that this quiet fascination with technology would one day lead me into a computer-related career. I had seen how machines worked, how they could break, how they could be fixed, and how they could open doors to new possibilities. In May 2024, I joined The Maasai National Polytechnic to pursue a Diploma in Information and Communication Technology (ICT). That decision opened a completely new chapter in my life. I began discovering the world of computers, programming, software, networks, databases, and digital innovation. Technology quickly became more than just a course I was studying; it became something I genuinely enjoyed. I started developing skills, building projects, experimenting with ideas, and discovering that I could use technology to solve problems. My time at the Maasai National Polytechnic allowed me to explore different sides of myself and discover abilities I had not fully recognized before.

The Maasai National Polytechnic also became a place where I developed beyond academics. I became actively involved in games and extracurricular activities, and I was fortunate enough to earn a medal through my participation. Music also became an important part of my journey. I participated in music activities and progressed all the way to the national level, representing and participating for three consecutive years. These experiences taught me discipline, teamwork, confidence, competition, and the importance of representing something bigger than myself. I also participated in a system-development competition, where we worked on developing a technological solution aimed at helping solve a problem in the country, and I was fortunate to earn another medal. These achievements reminded me that I was capable of competing, creating, performing, and succeeding in different areas. They also strengthened my belief that education should not only happen inside a classroom.

Leadership became another major part of my journey. Since primary school, I had often found myself taking leadership responsibilities, and at the Maasai National Polytechnic, this continued. I became a class representative, a role that allowed me to communicate with students, represent their concerns, and participate in discussions and decision-making. As time went on, my ambition grew. During my final year, I wanted to challenge myself further and become a leader at the institutional level. Initially, I had the ambition of vying for the presidency, but after considering the circumstances and the opportunities available to me, I decided to contest for the position of Cabinet Secretary for Academics. I campaigned, presented my ideas, interacted with students, and eventually won the election. I was officially crowned CS Academics.

Serving as CS Academics became one of the most memorable chapters of my life. I had the opportunity to attend meetings, participate in decision-making, listen to students, represent their interests, and contribute to discussions affecting academic life. The position taught me that leadership is not about a title; it is about responsibility, service, accountability, and being willing to listen to others. I enjoyed serving the students and working with different people who had different opinions, backgrounds, and expectations. The experience challenged me to become more confident in communication and decision-making. It also helped me understand that leadership comes with pressure, criticism, expectations, and the responsibility to remain focused even when things do not go exactly as planned. By the time my final term came to an end, I felt that I had gained much more than a leadership title. I had gained experiences and skills that I will carry into the next chapter of my life.

My journey through technology has also given me a new vision for the future. I have developed an interest in programming, web development, backend systems, databases, machine learning, and software development. I have worked on projects that challenged me to think about how technology can solve real-world problems. I have explored programming languages and technologies such as C, HTML, CSS, JavaScript, Python, FastAPI, and databases, among others. My interest is not simply to learn how to code but to understand how technology can be transformed into useful systems and businesses. I want to become someone who can identify a problem, understand it, design a solution, and use technology to bring that solution to life. My long-term ambition is to establish my own technology firm, where I can build innovative systems, create employment opportunities, and contribute to solving problems within my community and beyond.

Today, I am at a point where I am transitioning from student life into the professional world. I have completed my academic journey at the polytechnic and am now looking toward gaining practical experience through an internship in the technology field. This stage represents the beginning of a new chapter rather than the end of my education. I know that I still have a lot to learn, and I am willing to learn from experienced professionals, take on challenges, make mistakes, and improve. I want to use this opportunity to understand the professional environment and develop the skills required to become a competent technology professional. At the same time, I am thinking beyond employment. I want to build something of my own and eventually establish a technology firm that reflects my ideas, values, and vision.

When I look back at my life, I realize that my journey has never been an ordinary straight line. I started from a family that faced financial and personal challenges, grew up watching my father transform his life, struggled with school in my early years, repeated Class Two, feared a teacher, completed primary school with 318 marks, experienced the embarrassment of being mistaken for a girl because of my name, went through the challenges of secondary school, sat for KCSE, returned to school to improve my grade, changed my career ambition, joined a polytechnic, discovered technology, earned medals, participated at national level in music, competed in system development, became a class representative, and eventually became a student leader. Every chapter has contributed something to the person I am today. Some chapters gave me pain, some gave me happiness, some gave me embarrassment, and others gave me confidence, but all of them taught me something.

My story is therefore not simply a story about education or technology. It is a story about life, struggle, faith, transformation, ambition, and perseverance. I have learned that sometimes you can begin your journey without knowing whether you will make it to the end, but you must still take the first step with faith and hope. I have learned that failure does not have to be the final chapter, and that changing direction does not mean losing your purpose. I have learned from my father that transformation is possible, from my family that challenges can be survived, from school that education opens doors, from leadership that service requires responsibility, and from technology that ideas can become solutions. I carry all these lessons with me as I move forward.

My life is not just an ordinary life. It is a journey. A journey that began in Kikuvi Village, passed through challenges, classrooms, failures, victories, leadership, music, technology, and personal growth, and is now moving toward a future I am determined to create. I may not have started with everything, but I started with faith and hope. And as I continue forward, I carry one belief with me: my past may explain where I came from, but it will never decide where I am going.
